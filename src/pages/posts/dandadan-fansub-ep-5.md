![Over the shoulder of a schoolboy holding up a small figurine, looking across the street at Momo, who leans against a brick wall in a pink cardigan and school uniform.](/images/posts/dandadan-fansub-ep-5/image-1.png)

In this scene, Okarun is nervous about how he’s gonna say hi to Momo when he sees her before school. He’s surprised to find her waiting at the gate, and assumes she’s waiting for someone else. What does this mean? It means they *didn’t* prearrange to meet in the morning. Momo then says to him:

> Momo: 遅いし。行こう。

The sub and dub both translate 遅いし as “you’re late”. But he’s not late. In English, being “late” means that you had a time you were meant to show up, and you showed up later than that. There are two possible things he could be late for: seeing Momo, or class starting. He’s not late to seeing Momo because they clearly didn’t arrange to meet up that morning, and he’s not late to school because everyone is arriving at the same time, and no one is rushing. “You’re late” is an incorrect translation. It’s going to make the audience infer incorrect information, even if it is minor.

遅い has several meanings, and in this case it’s closer to “slow” than “late”. Momo is not accusing Okarun of *being* *late* to a prearranged time, but of arriving *later* than she expected (and indirectly telling him that she’s been waiting for him). But you can’t say “you’re slow” in English in this context, right? So how do you convey that idea in a natural way? I think “slowpoke” is one option, but it sounds lame as hell. “Where have you been?” comes off a little too presumptuous and irritated. “I’ve been waiting” is too ominous and directly conveys info that was deliberately indirect in the original script.

![Close up of Momo with red eyes and big mint green earrings, grinning confidently. The subtitle reads "Took your time."](/images/posts/dandadan-fansub-ep-5/image-2.png)

“Took your time” conveys almost everything 遅いし does, direct and indirect, including tone and familiarity! These are small things, but they really add up in terms of creating a natural sounding and accurate translation.

---

This next line gave me and Chy so much grief.

> Momo: 今日もイカしてるね、そのメガネ姿！バカ女にわかんないか、オカルンの魅力は。[side eyes Aira] **男落とすより、頭にタライ落としてる方がお似合いだぜ。**

The bolded line is a dunk on Aira, using a pun on 落とす (meaning “to fall” and “to make someone fall for you”). The dub had a decent direct translation: “They deserve washtubs falling on ‘em instead of guys falling *for* ‘em”. Not bad!

But this line is meant to be a sick burn, and because タライ (washpan) is not really a thing in English-speaking countries, the inclusion of an oddly specific and slightly unfamiliar word makes the roast sound a touch awkward. After a long time of trying to make it sound good, we figured the severity of the burn was more important to preserve than the specific mention of the washpan, which freed us up to make it sound a lot more savage. In the end, we had this:

> Momo: You look great as always! Those glasses really suit you. Some girls are too stupid to see it. [side eyes Aira] **They think they’re drop-dead gorgeous, but they actually just need to drop dead.**

![An extreme close up of Momo's narrowed red eye peering over someone's shoulder. The subtitle reads "They think they're drop-dead gorgeous, but they actually just need to drop dead."](/images/posts/dandadan-fansub-ep-5/image-3.png)

A final note on the Dandadan series — I love how Japanese this series is, even though it makes translating into natural English so much harder. It makes constant references to well-known (in Japan) actors and pop culture that most English speakers will have no chance of getting. This episode contains an extended bit where Seiko references Kinpachi Sensei, which was a very popular TV show about a kind but strict teacher who helped students with their life problems in order to teach them better. Since it’s highly unlikely that viewers of my translation are going to understand these references, in my translation I prioritise making it obvious that these jokes are references to something, so that the audience can dismiss it as a missed reference (rather than thinking they haven’t understood some plot detail) or even Google it themselves.

> Seiko: Don’t move, don’t speak, you bad apple.\
> Okarun: Wait, are you referencing Kinpachi Sensei?

> Seiko: 動くな喋るな腐ったみかん。\
> Okarun: ちょっと待って！それ完全にふざけてないですか？！

In the Japanese, Okarun doesn’t mention Kinpachi Sensei until his next line, but I thought it would make more sense to tell the English-speaking audience what was happening sooner so that Seiko’s next line (another non sequitur) has more context.
