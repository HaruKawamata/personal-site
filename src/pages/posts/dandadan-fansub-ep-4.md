I had to make another series-spanning decision this episode about how curse-Okarun refers to Momo. It needs to be very jarring compared to his usual way of referring to her, and intimate enough that Momo can be somewhat surprised and put out by it, because the first time he says it, she says:

> Momo: おい！気安く下の名前で呼ぶな！ (literally: Hey! Don’t call me by my first name like it’s nothing!)

The line alone showcases the social expectations between high schoolers (and between peers in general) in Japan quite well, I think. Here’s a simplified rundown.

- Lastname-san: use for peers you’re not super close with
- Lastname-chan: may use for girl peers you’re not super close with if they are known for having a cutesy vibe
- Lastname: use for boy peers, if you’re also a boy and you’re close or you see yourself as higher on the social rankings
- Firstname: use for close friends
- Firstname-chan: may use for your girlfriend or a girl you’re close with (comes with kinda flirty vibes if you’re gender-sexuality compatible)

Of course, there is more nuance than this, and this list is specifically for *peers* (an adult talking to a teen may say firstname-chan without it being flirty). But based on my understanding of the extremely presumptuous use of firstname-chan by Okarun here, I chose to translate it as “babe”, for the overt intimacy and the tonal whiplash from Okarun’s usual formality.

![Momo, bathed in red light, rides on the back of the Highway Hag-possessed Okarun against a starry night sky, gripping a rope. The subtitle reads "Whoa, what are you doing calling me "babe"?"](/images/posts/dandadan-fansub-ep-4/image-1.png)

This episode, I’m particularly proud of translating a mic-drop moment which involves some difficult-to-translate wordplay.

![A low angle shot of a figure standing between railway tracks under power lines hung with paper talismans, beneath a blood red sky. The subtitle reads "And it's the end of the line."](/images/posts/dandadan-fansub-ep-4/image-2.png)

> Momo: ばあちゃんの言った通りだぜ。煽れば必ず乗ってくる。てめえは乗っちまったんだぜ。負けのレールによ！

> Momo: Grandma was right. “If you challenge her, she’ll take the bait.” You took it hook, line and sinker. And it’s the end of the line.

The original line is making a play on words — 乗る can mean “to go along with” (e.g. a plan; the vibe; a taunt; a challenge) or “to literally get in/on a vehicle”. Momo taunted the Highway Hag into going along with her challenge, but also into getting on the train. I needed to find a pun that helped Momo’s victory moment feel as decisive and cool as it does in Japanese. Luckily, this was one of those eureka moments where your brain just comes up with something great straight away! My brain came up with a pun on the word “line” — being a fishing line for “baiting” and the railway line.

There’s a scene where Momo uses the word リスペクト (risupekuto: respect) which makes the Highway Hag go off on a rant about damn kids and their English loanwords, and ironically finishes the rant with an English insult サンオブアビッチ (transcribed: san obu a bitchi).

Even in the Japanese, Momo is using much more slang than usual (though “respect” is the only loanword she uses), so I chose to make Highway Hag’s rant about youth slang instead of the excessive English usage by Japanese teens. Importantly, her final line has to ironically use some teen slang herself.

> Highway Hag: Speak properly you little shit! I know what you’re doing. Trying to confuse me with your teenage slang. Awful, just awful stuff. I can’t stand kids like you. Hiding behind those stupid phrases! You’re gonna make me… Crash the fuck out!

![A monstrous, giant version of the Highway Hag looms over a tiny figure under a red-lit overpass, her face covered in a glowing yellow net. The subtitle, in a spooky serif font, reads "Crash the fuck out!"](/images/posts/dandadan-fansub-ep-4/image-3.png)

Side note, but I thought it was interesting that the anime script usually doesn’t deviate from the manga’s writing at all, but in this case the manga’s original line was ファッキンビッチ (fakkin bitchi). Usually Japanese speakers aren’t super concerned with the intensity of swear words from English, so I wonder how much of this change came from English-speaking Netflix executives. I also wonder if this script change would have still happened if the official English subs did something more like what I did here and changed the irony from an English thing to a teenage slang thing, since old people hating on loanwords isn’t really a thing in English. As it stands, the official subs half-ass it — they don’t translate the “speak Japanese, stop trying to confuse me with English” part literally, opting for a translation about “modern words”. But then they do a literal translation of this particular line (“What a son of a bitch”), completely missing the point of that line!
