This has absolutely been the toughest episode yet. It has several difficult phrases, the most dialogue of any episode up to this point, and a few phases that are really important to future episodes and core themes.

## This episode’s translation discussion

> ねえ、じゅり<span class="color-blue">先輩</span>

> Can I ask you something, Juri?

The bane of every translator’s existence — <span class="color-blue">先輩</span> (senpai). As I’m doing with 様 (sama), I definitely don’t want to include it untranslated as an honorific, but unlike 様 (sama) there’s no English word that could possibly be used in the same context to convey similar information. Instead, I’m trying to slip in some of the hierarchy in the characters’ general speech patterns. Here, the translation invents a whole sentence for Miki to show a bit of deference to Juri, instead of using the honorific.

—–

> <span class="color-red">テクニックでねじ伏せるようないつもの迫力</span>じゃなくて、<span class="color-blue">なんだか音に潤いを感じるよ</span>。

> <span class="color-red">Your technique is usually so forceful and commanding</span>, but today <span class="color-blue">the notes have a kind of richness to them</span>.

According to [jisho.org](http://jisho.org/), <span class="color-red">ねじ伏せる</span> means “to twist someone’s arm and throw them to the ground”. <span class="color-red">迫力</span> means “force, impact, strength”. Touga’s verbiage here is intentionally violent. It indicates that underneath Miki’s polite, almost feminine demeanour and appearance, lies a boy who is already being moulded by patriarchal forces into a force of oppression. It was important to keep that hint of Miki’s potential for violence and oppression in the translation.

—–

> 前に言ってた、<span class="color-orange">輝くもの</span>でも見つけたのかな？

> Did you ever find that <span class="color-orange">“glow”</span> you were talking about earlier? (from [ohtori.nu](http://ohtori.nu/))

> It seems you may have found that <span class="color-orange">guiding light</span> you were telling me about. (my translation)

This was a really tough translation, and one that has implications for the future, because I believe <span class="color-orange">輝くもの</span> is used when listing the council’s reasons for duelling in later episodes, so the translation I chose needed to fit in that context as well.

Depending on the kanji used, もの can mean either “thing” (物) or “person” (者). A Japanese transcript I found actually uses the “person” kanji excusively. So it could be translated as either “<span class="color-orange">something that shines</span>” or “<span class="color-orange">someone that shines</span>”. I think I managed to preserve this ambiguity in the translation: “<span class="color-orange">guiding light</span>” could be a literal light, or it could be a metaphor for a person who’s very important to you.

—–

> <span class="color-blue">自分の中に</span><span class="color-purple">永遠の</span><span class="color-pink">美しさ</span><span class="color-blue">を持ってない</span><span class="color-orange">と引けない曲なんだ</span>

> It’s an ode to <span class="color-pink">a beautiful life</span>, <span class="color-purple">where things never change</span>. <span class="color-orange">You need</span> <span class="color-blue">to believe in that</span> <span class="color-orange">to play it</span>.

The Japanese literally translates to “<span class="color-orange">This is a song \[you/I\] can’t play</span> <span class="color-blue">unless \[you/I\] carry</span> \[<span class="color-purple">eternal</span> <span class="color-pink">beauty</span>/the <span class="color-pink">beauty</span> of <span class="color-purple">eternity</span>\] <span class="color-blue">within yourself</span>”. The subject of the sentence is omitted - is he talking in general or specifically about himself? Is <span class="color-purple">永遠</span> here acting as a noun (<span class="color-purple">eternity</span>) and の marks <span class="color-pink">美しさ</span> as belonging to it (<span class="color-pink">beauty</span> of <span class="color-purple">eternity</span>) or is it acting in the genitive case and modifying the noun <span class="color-pink">美しさ</span> instead (<span class="color-purple">eternal</span> <span class="color-pink">beauty</span>)?

These are all questions that matter in terms of making sense of the line, but not in the immediate sense of the translation, because the translation pivots on a different axis: <span class="color-blue">自分の中に…持ってないと</span>. In english, translating this literally (<span class="color-blue">carry … inside yourself</span>) sounds unnatural. I think the Japanese gives more of an impression of carrying a worldview of eternal beauty; of seeing the world as a world that has eternal beauty. Textually, what Miki is talking about is carrying a dream of reliving the happiness of the past again, and then keeping it forever. He’s desperately trying to preserve that perfect time in his life, with him and his sister side by side playing that song in the sunlit garden. That’s *actually* what this is about. And his duelling loss, and the scene where the image of the garden shatters, represents him finally being forced to move on from that need for permanence.

Permanence (or as most translations have it, “<span class="color-purple">eternity</span>” — <span class="color-purple">永遠</span>) is a core theme of the work. All of the characters, in some way, are trying to preserve a form of status quo, or regain and retain a status quo that has been lost to them. Though Miki uses the word <span class="color-purple">永遠</span> here, I decided not to directly translate it. Rather than relying on the recurrence of a single noun to carry this theme, I wanted to allow the theme to stand on its own merit. I think translating it variously as “<span class="color-purple">permanence</span>”, “<span class="color-purple">eternity</span>” and other edge cases as I’ve done in this line (“<span class="color-purple">where things never change</span>”), will allow me to better convey this theme of wanting to keep things as they are, and the necessity of change and of letting those things go.
