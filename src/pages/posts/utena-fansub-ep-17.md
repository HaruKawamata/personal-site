We’re back! Apologies for the hiatus, Christmas and Oshōgatsu had me too busy with family stuff to stick to my usual rate of translation.

![Shiori, a girl with short purple hair in a sailor uniform, smiles faintly inside an ornate black wrought-iron frame with a peach-coloured rose in each corner.](/images/posts/utena-fansub-ep-17/image-1.png)

This time, instead of general translation notes, I’d like to show my working! Where do I start with a tough line, and how to I end up on the translations I settle on?

> いつも私を<span class="color-orange">守って</span>くれた

For example, this is an interesting line, and the final translation is a bit out there if you look at it without seeing the thought process. The key word here is <span class="color-orange">守る</span>, which is often translated as “<span class="color-orange">protect</span>”, so the lowest-hanging fruit for this line would be:

> She always <span class="color-orange">protected</span> me.

But it sounds weird and conspicuously like “anime-english” which I always try desperately to avoid. So I tried this instead:

> She always <span class="color-orange">took care</span> of me.

This translation gets the literal meaning across better, but it has a fatal flaw. The word <span class="color-orange">守る</span> has strong connotations with masculinity in general and throughout the series, yet “taking care of” someone makes it sound too feminine. In this line, it’s important that Juri’s masculinity is emphasised and contrasted against Shiori’s femininity. So then I thought, maybe this:

> She was always <span class="color-orange">protective</span> of me.

But this is no good either. The くれた in <span class="color-orange">守って</span>くれた makes it clear that Shiori views the care/protection that Juri provided as a positive thing, yet this translation comes across as slightly bitter. In English I think “being protective of someone” has the nuance of restricting one’s freedom, like an overbearing parent.

I felt like there were no good options for this line! Then it struck me like lightning:

> She was my rock. (no <span class="color-orange">orange</span> because I didn’t use an equivalent word at all)

“Being someone’s rock” is such a masculine thing! It also carries these patriarchal notions of stability (masc) vs instability (fem), which I think the series tries quite hard to weave into the story as criticisms of patriarchal gender. Juri at least in part represents women who seek success not through liberation for all women, but by becoming a patriarchal dominator herself.

Not to mention, <span class="color-orange">守る</span> is often depicted in RGU as being something that a man does to their female lover. The “my rock” translation also carries romantic connotations, which is obviously important for how the Shiori/Juri relationship is portrayed through the dialogue.

This translation departs from the literal meaning quite heavily, but it preserves the subtextual meaning much better, which I think is more important in this case.

![](/images/posts/shared/rose-divider.png)

> あの<span class="color-purple">動き</span>、まるで先輩の<span class="color-red">剣</span>だ。

> The way she <span class="color-purple">moves</span>… <span class="color-red">it’s</span> exactly like Juri. (my initial translation, which I didn’t use in the end)

The word <span class="color-red">剣</span> means (literally) <span class="color-red">sword</span>, but it can also be used metaphorically to refer to someone’s sword <i><span class="color-red">technique</span></i>. Take this panel from Ranking of Kings:

![A black-and-white manga panel from Ranking of Kings: a stern long-haired man raises his hand mid-lesson. His speech bubbles read "それは王の剣じゃない" and "しっかり打ち合いなさい！"](/images/posts/utena-fansub-ep-17/image-2.jpg)

> それは王の<span class="color-red">剣</span>じゃない！

> That’s no <span class="color-red">way</span> for a king <span class="color-red">to fight</span>!

> That’s not the “<span class="color-red">sword</span>” of a king!

So the question is — how do you translate this when the swords are both literal AND metaphorical? When Utena says <span class="color-red">剣</span> in this line, they’re talking about the technique (<span class="color-red">剣</span> as a metaphor). But the writers want us to make the connection that it is also *literally* Juri’s sword, pulled out of her heart, which in turn is a metaphor for her anger, insecurities, and angst. It’s a metaphor for swordsmanship, which is meant to be interpreted as literal through dramatic irony, which literal meaning is meant to be reinterpreted as a deeper metaphor. I love Utena.

If the swordfights can be interpreted as philosophical debates, the swords can be considered the worldview that each character wields. Since the Black Rose duellists don’t use their own swords, they could be said to be tapping into, stealing, and exploiting the worldviews/swords of their respective victims, represented by their literal ripping the sword out of the victim’s 心 (heart/mind).

With no word in English that can capture the metaphorical triple meaning of <span class="color-red">剣</span>, I had to settle for something else:

> Those <span class="color-purple">moves</span>… it’s like she stole them from Juri. (no <span class="color-red">red</span> because I didn’t use an equivalent word at all)

I think this phrasing gets across a similar feel that <span class="color-red">剣</span> does. The base reading, and the one that the uninformed and naive Utena intends, is that Shiori is just imitating, “stealing”, Juri’s moves. The next level up is that she literally did steal something from Juri — the sword from her heart. Then the final level is that what she stole was not just a physical sword; she *literally* stole the *metaphorical* moves by stealing the sword, because the moves are the sword, the sword is the angst, the angst is the worldview!
